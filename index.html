From home kitchen to trusted postpartum brand.

A low-overhead launch plan for cookies and quick breads—built around direct sales, careful claims, and the local relationships that already gather new mothers.

THE RECOMMENDATION
Start narrow. Sell direct. Let trust compound.

Use the home-based-vendor route, earn the food-handler certificate, validate at local markets, and postpone wholesale until a commercial kitchen makes sense.

01 / THESIS

The business in one page

Knead & Nurse can begin as a compliant home bakery with unusually light regulatory overhead. The strategic edge is not a promise about milk supply; it is Beth’s empathy for postpartum life, translated into a convenient, thoughtful food experience.

LEGAL FIT
Home production is viable.

Non-TCS cookies and quick breads qualify. Under the recommended HBV route, direct online and phone sales plus in-state delivery or shipping are available with an ANSI food-handler certificate.[1]

FIRST CHANNEL
Markets + preorder.

Use Avon as the local trust engine, then nearby markets and permission-based community partners.

PRIMARY RISK
Health claims.

The safest positioning is descriptive—ingredients, taste, convenience, care—not supply outcomes.[4]

Launch posture

One tightly managed cookie offer plus one quick-bread offer; direct to the end customer; limited order windows Beth can run alongside nursing shifts.

Brand posture

“Nurse” should evoke feeding and caring for a child. Beth’s L&D background can support the founder story—but never imply clinical endorsement or use IU Health branding.

02 / LEGAL ROUTE

Choose the lane before printing labels

Indiana now has two overlapping paths. The home-based-vendor lane is the better fit for preorders, online sales, delivery, and Indiana shipping. The newer homestead-vendor lane is useful for property and farmers-market sales. An ANSI certificate keeps both paths open.

HBV · IC 16-42-5.3

Best when Beth wants direct online or phone ordering, local delivery, and shipping within Indiana. Cookies and quick breads fit when they are non-TCS—foods that do not require temperature control for safety.[1]

SALESDirect to consumer, including online and phone
DELIVERYIndiana only; sealed, tamper-evident packaging
CERTIFICATEANSI food-handler certificate required
REQUIRED LABEL · ALL TEXT AT LEAST 10-POINT
  • Producer name and address
  • Product name
  • Ingredients in descending order by weight
  • Net weight or volume
  • Date of processing
“This product is home produced and processed and the production area has not been inspected by the state department of health. NOT FOR RESALE.”

Post the full label online for each product. Keep Indiana shipping records for one year. No out-of-state shipping and no wholesale.

Homestead vendor · IC 16-42-5.4

Best for sales on Beth’s property and at farmers markets, directly to the end consumer. The law covers prepared foods including baked goods and sets a $1.5 million annual gross-sales cap.[2]

SALESOwn property or farmers market
DELIVERYIndiana only, with certificate and sealed package
CERTIFICATERequired only for shipping or delivery
REQUIRED LABEL · DISCLAIMER AT LEAST 10-POINT
  • Vendor name and address
  • Product description
  • Ingredients in descending order by weight
  • Description of allergens that may have contacted the food
“This product was produced by a homestead vendor or the owner of a small farm that is exempt from government licensing and inspection.”

No wholesale. State and local health bodies may still investigate foodborne-illness complaints.

03 / LAUNCH

Ten steps to first sale

The sequence matters: resolve the open local and claims questions first, then spend on packaging and market commitments.

Confirm the name and claims boundary

Before packaging, have a food-labeling attorney review use of “lactation” and all milk-supply-adjacent wording. Keep the launch copy descriptive until that review is complete.

OUTPUT · APPROVED MESSAGE BANK

Call Avon Planning & Building

Ask whether a home-occupation permit applies to a home bakery at Beth’s address and whether customer pickup, signage, parking, or delivery traffic changes the answer. Published requirements and any fee could not be verified. Call (317) 272-0948.[3]

OUTPUT · WRITTEN ZONING ANSWER

Check private restrictions

Review any HOA covenants for home-business, signage, traffic, or pickup restrictions. Local approval does not override private covenants.

OUTPUT · HOA CLEARANCE

Earn the ANSI food-handler certificate

Budget roughly $10–$40 depending on provider and format. Keep the certificate available and provide it to the county health department on request. The certificate unlocks HBV sales and homestead-vendor delivery/shipping.

OUTPUT · CERTIFICATE ON FILE

Form the operating shell

Recommended: file an Indiana LLC online for $95, designate an Indiana registered agent, and adopt an operating agreement. A single-member LLC generally flows through Schedule C; it does not protect Beth from her own food-preparation negligence, so insurance still matters.

OUTPUT · LLC + SEPARATE RECORDS

Bind product liability coverage

Target coverage that satisfies Avon’s $1M general liability, $1M product liability, and $2M aggregate requirement, with the Town added as certificate holder/additional insured when applying.[5]

OUTPUT · COI READY

Resolve sales tax and register if required

Ask Indiana DOR how Beth’s packaged take-home products are classified. Indiana’s general rate is 7%; groceries are generally exempt, while prepared/ready-to-eat food is taxable. Avon’s handbook tells vendors to obtain an RRMC and collect on ready-to-eat items. RRMC registration is $25 per location.[6]

OUTPUT · DOR ANSWER + RRMC

Lock recipes, allergens, and labels

Document the final recipe by weight, identify every allergen and cross-contact risk, choose the governing label, confirm at least 10-point type, and post the full HBV label anywhere the product is sold online.

OUTPUT · PRINT-READY LABELS

Run a paid pilot

Open a small preorder window to a permission-based local audience. Track orders, channel, repeat interest, production time, ingredient and packaging cost, and customer comments about taste and convenience—not supply outcomes.

OUTPUT · REAL DEMAND SIGNAL

Apply to the market, then repeat what works

Start with an Avon single-day or monthly commitment before a full season. Use each event to drive the next preorder window and measure which product, story, and pickup format earns repeat orders.

05 / GO TO MARKET

A 90-day learning loop

The goal of the first quarter is not maximum reach. It is proof that a small, compliant offer can turn local trust into repeat direct orders without overwhelming Beth’s clinical schedule.

DAYS 1–30 · FOUNDATION

Make the offer legible

  • Resolve zoning, food-handler, insurance, tax, and claims questions.
  • Finalize one cookie and one quick-bread offer.
  • Create compliant labels and one clear preorder page.
  • Build production slots around Beth’s work schedule.
DAYS 31–60 · PROOF

Sell in a tight circle

  • Run a paid, limited preorder window.
  • Use local pickup or Indiana delivery under the chosen legal lane.
  • Book a single Avon market day.
  • Capture permission for future order-window notices.
DAYS 61–90 · REPEAT

Scale the winning motion

  • Repeat the best-performing product and channel.
  • Ask satisfied buyers for taste- and service-based reviews.
  • Approach doulas, pediatric offices, and peer-support leaders for permission-based referrals.
  • Test a monthly market commitment before a season pass.
ACQUIRE
Farmers markets

Face-to-face sampling and story. Avon is the first test; Brownsburg and Danville create a workable local weekly rhythm.

CONVERT
Limited preorder windows

Clear cutoff, pickup or delivery method, full product label online, and a capacity cap that respects Beth’s shift schedule.

REFER
Permission-based professionals

Doulas, WIC breastfeeding peer counselors, pediatric offices, and support-group leaders can share a neutral resource if they choose. No hard-selling in care settings.

RETAIN
Post-purchase follow-up

Invite a reorder and ask about flavor, texture, packaging, and convenience. Do not solicit or publish testimonials about milk-supply changes.

07 / CLAIMS

Sell comfort, not an outcome

A June 2026 federal complaint challenges common lactation-cookie claims. It is an allegation—not a ruling—but it makes conservative wording the smart launch posture.[4]

✓ Safer direction

  • Describe the product, ingredients, flavor, texture, size, and packaging.
  • Position around care, nourishment, convenience, gifting, and postpartum days without promising a physiological result.
  • Use taste- and service-based customer feedback.
  • Make allergen and cross-contact information easy to find.

× Avoid at launch

  • “Increases,” “boosts,” “enhances,” or “supports” milk supply.
  • “Clinically proven,” “#1,” or “lactation consultant recommended.”
  • Efficacy-framed “traditionally used” wording.
  • Testimonials claiming milk-supply improvement.
  • Disease, treatment, or therapeutic claims.
Founder-story rule: Beth may accurately say she is a labor-and-delivery nurse. Keep that biography separate from efficacy claims, employer endorsement, clinical advice, and IU Health marks. Her experience should shape empathy and service—not serve as evidence that the food changes milk supply.
08 / OPERATIONS

Build a tiny system before a big audience

The early operating model should make every order traceable, every label reproducible, and every sales window intentionally capacity-limited.

Product file

  • Master recipe by weight
  • Ingredient supplier and lot notes
  • Allergen and cross-contact review
  • Final label proof and processing date
  • Packaging and tamper-evidence check

Order file

  • Customer order and fulfillment method
  • Product and processing date
  • Pickup, delivery, or shipment completion
  • Shipping records retained one year under HBV rules
  • Issue, refund, or complaint notes

Price from the work backward

Do not copy a competitor’s price. Record the full cost of each batch, including Beth’s time and the cost of packaging, then divide by sellable units before choosing a price.

UNIT COST = (INGREDIENTS + PACKAGING + BATCH LABOR + ALLOCATED FEES) ÷ SELLABLE UNITS

Protect schedule capacity

Use defined order windows instead of always-on availability. Close the window when the planned production capacity is reached; do not make clinical shifts compete with rush orders.

WINDOW CAPACITY = AVAILABLE PRODUCTION TIME ÷ VERIFIED TIME PER ORDER
09 / GROWTH GATES

Grow only when the next constraint is visible

Each stage should solve a proven bottleneck. Do not take on wholesale, a storefront, or a broader product catalog simply because the business feels busy.

GATE 01
Repeatability

Advance when the same products can be produced, labeled, sold, and fulfilled consistently across multiple order windows.

STAY HOME-BASED
GATE 02
Channel fit

Advance when one local channel produces repeat orders—not just event-day transactions—and Beth can identify why buyers return.

ADD MARKET CADENCE
GATE 03
Capacity

Advance when verified demand repeatedly exceeds safe production capacity. Consider a licensed commercial kitchen only after that constraint is measured.

EVALUATE KITCHEN
GATE 04
Wholesale readiness

Only a licensed commercial-kitchen path can unlock resale through baby boutiques or hospital gift shops. Hospital sampling or solicitation also requires facility approval.
